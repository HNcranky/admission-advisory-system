Chapter~\ref{chapter:Experiment} presented the system as a whole.
This chapter isolates its principal contributions and examines each in the form problem, solution, and result.
Three contributions address gaps that existing admission tools leave open, as identified in Chapter~\ref{chapter:Related_works}: the conflict-aware consolidation of contradictory source data (Section~\ref{section:5.1}), the resilient inference gateway that keeps the assistant available under model failures (Section~\ref{section:5.2}), and the end-to-end ingestion of heterogeneous official sources including scanned documents (Section~\ref{section:5.3}).
Three further contributions concern the engineering of a maintainable and economical \gls{LLM} application: unified observability over every advisory run (Section~\ref{section:5.4}), the declarative orchestration of the conversation and knowledge flows as reusable graphs (Section~\ref{section:5.5}), and a version-gated semantic cache that reuses past answers (Section~\ref{section:5.6}).

\section{Conflict-aware data consolidation}
\label{section:5.1}

\textbf{Problem.}
Official admission data is not only fragmented but internally inconsistent: two authoritative publications for the same program can state different quotas, and two sources can record different historical cutoff scores for the same program and year.
As established in Chapter~\ref{chapter:Related_works}, every existing channel resolves such disagreement silently --- it picks one value or shows none --- which hides exactly the uncertainty a student needs to know about before committing an application.
Consolidating these sources therefore cannot mean overwriting one value with another; the disagreement must survive into the recommendation.
The general problem of combining records that describe the same entity but disagree on attribute values is studied as \textit{data fusion}~\cite{bleiholder2009fusion}, which frames the available strategies as resolving, ignoring, or deferring a conflict; the design below applies the deferring strategy where a conflict cannot be settled objectively.
The related field of \textit{truth discovery}~\cite{li2016truth} estimates which of several conflicting values is correct by jointly inferring per-source reliability, and its central finding --- that no single source can be trusted unconditionally and that reliability must be inferred from evidence --- motivates the conservative stance taken here: the system resolves a disagreement only when an objective, auditable signal makes one value defensible, and otherwise defers rather than commits to a possibly wrong winner.

\textbf{Solution.}
Conflict awareness is built in three layers, and the detection-and-resolution logic is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:conflict}.
At the storage layer, as described in Section~\ref{section:4.2}, the canonical tables are unique per source, so contradictory publications coexist as distinct rows rather than overwriting one another.
At the detection layer, the conflict service groups candidate records by program, year, and method --- the canonical conflict key --- and flags a conflict whenever the grouped rows carry two or more distinct values; quota and cutoff conflicts are detected separately because they have different keys and different resolution rules.
At the resolution layer, the two kinds of conflict are treated deliberately differently.
A quota conflict is settled by a deterministic comparison that ranks the competing options on a fixed lexicographic ordering of trust level, then corroboration, then recency, then source confidence.
If that comparison is decisive --- the top option strictly outranks its nearest differing challenger on at least one axis --- the highest-ranked value is accepted; if every axis ties, the conflict is left unresolved rather than guessed, the field is marked uncertain, and every competing value is surfaced.
No language model is consulted at any point in conflict resolution; the conflict agent wires only these deterministic comparators, which keeps the resolution fully auditable.
The ranking is intentionally lexicographic rather than a weighted score, so that the axis on which a decision turns is always recoverable: the comparison reports the first axis where the winner and its nearest differing challenger diverge --- trust level, then corroboration, then recency, then declared confidence --- and that axis is stored on the outcome.
The second axis, corroboration, counts how many independent sources report the same value, which is the operational form of the truth-discovery intuition that agreement across sources is itself evidence~\cite{li2016truth}; a value asserted by two official publications outranks an equally trusted value asserted by one.
Only when none of these axes separates the candidates --- equal trust, equal corroboration, equal recency, equal confidence --- does the comparison declare itself non-decisive, and only then is the value withheld.
A cutoff conflict is never resolved by ranking alone either.
Instead, the system checks whether the disagreement is \textit{decision-changing} for this particular student --- that is, whether the competing cutoff values, classified by the cutoff-assessment service, would place the student's score into different fit categories.
If it is, the conflict is left unresolved, the affected field is marked uncertain, and the explanation presents every competing value; if it is not --- or if the student's score or method is not on a comparable scale --- the system references the most trusted value while still stating all of them.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Conflict detection and resolution (per recommended program)}
\label{alg:conflict}
\KwIn{candidate records $R$ for a program; student score $s$ and method $m$}
\KwOut{a resolved value, or an uncertainty annotation listing all competing values}
Group $R$ by the conflict key $(\textit{program}, \textit{year}, \textit{method})$, separately for quota and cutoff fields\;
\ForEach{group $g$ with $\geq 2$ distinct quota values}{
  rank the options of $g$ by $(\textit{trust}, \textit{corroboration}, \textit{recency}, \textit{confidence})$, descending\;
  \eIf{top option strictly outranks its nearest differing challenger on some axis}{
    resolve quota to the top-ranked value\;
  }{
    mark quota \textbf{uncertain}; surface every competing value\;
  }
}
\ForEach{group $g$ with $\geq 2$ distinct cutoff scores}{
  order the options of $g$ by trust level (then score)\;
  \eIf{$s$ known \textbf{and} $m$ on a comparable scale}{
    $F \leftarrow \{\,\textsc{ClassifyFit}(s, v) : v \in \textit{values}(g)\,\}$\;
    \eIf{$|F| > 1$ \tcp*[r]{decision-changing}}{
      mark cutoff \textbf{uncertain}; list every competing value\;
    }{
      reference the most-trusted value; still list all values\;
    }
  }{
    reference the most-trusted value; still list all values\;
  }
}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Result.}
The pipeline surfaces conflicts instead of hiding them.
When a program's sources disagree in a way that matters, the recommendation marks the field as uncertain, lists the differing values, and advises the student to verify against the latest official announcement, which is the behavior required by edge case EC-16 and the conflict-disclosure use case of Chapter~\ref{chapter:Related_works}.
The conservative division of labor --- a fully deterministic comparison that resolves a quota conflict only when one value objectively outranks the others, and a decision-changing test that refuses to settle a cutoff conflict whenever the choice would alter the student's fit category --- keeps every consolidation auditable and defers, rather than guesses, exactly the cases where an over-confident commitment could mislead a student.
The conflict-handling edge cases (EC-16 and EC-17) are among the 17 satisfied cases reported in Section~\ref{section:4.4}.

\section{Resilient LLM inference gateway}
\label{section:5.2}

\textbf{Problem.}
The assistant depends on a hosted \gls{LLM} reached over the network, and that dependency fails in two distinct ways.
The first is a \textit{hard failure}: the \gls{API} is unreachable, unauthenticated, or rate-limited, which is common because the inference quota is finite and shared across every reasoning step.
The second is a \textit{soft failure}: the call succeeds but the body cannot be used --- the model wraps its \gls{JSON} in prose, emits malformed syntax, or returns a value outside the expected set.
A naive implementation that treats every \gls{LLM} call as reliable would let either failure crash a consultation, violating the graceful-degradation requirement NFR-01.
The two failure kinds also call for opposite responses: retrying a rate-limited endpoint is pointless, whereas retrying a malformed structured response can succeed.

\textbf{Solution.}
All model access is funneled through a single gateway (Section~\ref{section:4.1}) that makes the two failure modes first-class; its control flow is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:gateway}.
A hard failure is raised as an \texttt{InferenceError}; a soft failure is reported on the result as a \texttt{failure\_type} of \texttt{STRUCTURE\_FAILURE} rather than as an exception.
The gateway resolves a per-agent policy from a model registry that supplies the primary model, an optional fallback model, a retry budget, and whether fallback is allowed for that agent.
It then treats the two failures differently: on a hard error it stops retrying the same model immediately and moves to the configured fallback model, because retrying an unreachable endpoint wastes time; on a structure failure it retries within the per-agent retry budget and then falls back to the secondary model.
If no fallback is configured, the gateway re-raises the hard error so that the call site can degrade, and every call site is written to do exactly that --- wrapping the call and substituting deterministic output on failure.
Whether a fallback exists, and how many structure retries precede it, is not global but per agent: the registry resolves an \texttt{InferencePolicy} for the calling agent, supplying its primary model, an optional fallback model, a retry budget, and an explicit \texttt{allow\_fallback} flag that defaults to off.
This lets a cheap, high-volume step such as intent classification run with no fallback and degrade to deterministic keywords, while a step whose output is harder to reconstruct can be granted a secondary model --- the resilience budget is spent where it is worth spending.
Every attempt is reported to a telemetry sink with its model, attempt number, resolved \texttt{failure\_type}, and a \texttt{used\_fallback} flag, so a degraded call is never silent: an operator can see that a stage fell back or that a structure failure recurred, which is the observability half of graceful degradation.
The clearest instance of this discipline is intent classification.
When the gateway is unavailable or returns an unusable response, the router falls back to a deterministic keyword classifier that mirrors the prompt's own priority rules: it checks knowledge-topic keywords before advisory phrases, so a factual question is never misrouted into a full advisory run while the model is unavailable, and unrecognized messages become a clarification request rather than a silent re-run.
This deterministic fallback was introduced in commit \texttt{c2ef582}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Inference gateway: hard- vs structure-failure handling}
\label{alg:gateway}
\KwIn{request $q$ with an agent name}
\KwOut{a usable \texttt{InferenceResult}, or a raised \texttt{InferenceError} for the call site to handle}
$p \leftarrow$ registry policy for $q$'s agent \tcp*[r]{primary, fallback, retries, allow\_fallback}
$e \leftarrow \varnothing$ \tcp*[r]{remembered hard error}
\For{$\text{attempt} \leftarrow 0$ \KwTo $p.\textit{maxRetries}$}{
  $r \leftarrow \textsc{Generate}(q, p.\textit{primaryModel})$\;
  \If{\textsc{Generate} raised \texttt{InferenceError} $\varepsilon$ \tcp*[r]{hard: network/auth/rate-limit}}{
    $e \leftarrow \varepsilon$; record \textsc{ApiError}; \textbf{break} \tcp*[r]{stop retrying this model}
  }
  record $r.\textit{failureType}$\;
  \lIf{$r.\textit{failureType} \neq \textsc{StructureFailure}$}{\Return $r$}
}
\eIf{$p.\textit{allowFallback}$ \textbf{and} $p.\textit{fallbackModel}$ exists}{
  $r \leftarrow \textsc{Generate}(q, p.\textit{fallbackModel})$ \tcp*[r]{re-raises on hard error}
  \Return $r$\;
}{
  \lIf{$e \neq \varnothing$}{\textbf{raise} $e$ \tcp*[r]{let the call site degrade}}
  \Return $r$ \tcp*[r]{last structure-failure result}
}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Result.}
The assistant remains usable through model outages.
Intent routing continues to function without any model call, knowledge answering degrades to an honest ``no data'' reply rather than a fabricated one, and structured-output hiccups are absorbed by retry and fallback instead of surfacing as crashes.
Concentrating this logic in one gateway means every reasoning step in the system --- routing, conflict tie-breaking, knowledge answering, and \gls{OCR} --- inherits the same resilience without re-implementing it, and the per-stage telemetry records when a fallback or a structure failure occurred, so an outage is visible to operators rather than silent.

\section{End-to-end ingestion of heterogeneous official sources}
\label{section:5.3}

\textbf{Problem.}
The data that makes the assistant useful lives in sources that share no common format: each university structures its admission pages differently, proposal documents are published as \gls{PDF}s, and some of those \gls{PDF}s are scanned images with no text layer at all.
A pipeline that assumes uniform, machine-readable input cannot ingest this material, yet without it the canonical store and the knowledge corpus would be empty.
Scanned documents are the hardest case, because recovering their text requires \gls{OCR}, and model-based \gls{OCR} introduces a failure mode of its own.

\textbf{Solution.}
Ingestion is a configurable, staged pipeline (Section~\ref{section:4.1}): per-source configuration selects the fetch strategy, the document router predicts each document's type, and a matching parser and extractor turn it into raw facts that are then normalized against per-school dictionaries into canonical records.
Because the per-source configuration and dictionaries are data rather than code, adding a school is largely a matter of configuration.
Scanned \gls{PDF}s are handled by a hybrid per-page extractor (Section~\ref{section:3.4}), whose logic is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ocr}: a page whose stripped text layer is at least 50 characters long is read directly, and only shorter (effectively image-only) pages are rendered to PNG at roughly 200 \gls{DPI} and sent to the multimodal gateway for transcription, keeping cost proportional to the number of scanned pages.
The distinctive part is the safeguard against model \gls{OCR} repetition loops, introduced in commit \texttt{b41dd9f}.
When transcribing a page with merged table cells, the model can lock into emitting one character endlessly --- one NEU page produced on the order of a million dash characters.
The extractor classifies an output as \textit{degenerate} when it exceeds an absolute ceiling of 20{,}000 characters per page, or when --- among at least 2{,}000 non-whitespace characters --- a single character accounts for more than 80\% of them.
A degenerate page is retried once at a raised decoding temperature (0.3, up from 0.0) to break the greedy loop; if it is still degenerate the page is marked failed and the remaining pages of the document are ingested normally, so one bad page cannot discard a whole document, while a document that yields no text at all is deliberately left un-ingested rather than recorded as empty.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Hybrid per-page extraction with degenerate-OCR detection}
\label{alg:ocr}
\KwIn{a scanned or mixed \gls{PDF}}
\KwOut{per-page text, each tagged \texttt{text\_layer}, \texttt{ocr}, or \texttt{failed}}
\ForEach{page in document}{
  $b \leftarrow$ stripped pdfplumber text layer of the page\;
  \lIf{$|b| \geq 50$}{record $(b, \texttt{text\_layer})$; \textbf{continue}}
  $\textit{img} \leftarrow$ render the page to PNG at $\approx 200$ \gls{DPI}\;
  \For{$\tau \in (0.0,\ 0.3)$ \tcp*[r]{retry once at raised temperature}}{
    $t \leftarrow \textsc{GatewayOCR}(\textit{img},\ \tau)$\;
    \lIf{\textbf{not} \textsc{Degenerate}$(t)$}{record $(t, \texttt{ocr})$; \textbf{continue to next page}}
  }
  record $(\texttt{""}, \texttt{failed})$ \tcp*[r]{both attempts degenerate}
}
\lIf{every page is empty}{\textbf{raise} --- leave the document un-ingested}
\BlankLine
\SetKwProg{Fn}{Function}{:}{}
\Fn{\textsc{Degenerate}$(t)$}{
  \lIf{$|t| > 20000$}{\Return true}
  $c \leftarrow t$ with all whitespace removed\;
  \lIf{$|c| \geq 2000$ \textbf{and} $\max_x \text{count}(x, c) / |c| > 0.8$}{\Return true}
  \Return false\;
}
\end{algorithm}

Two properties of this control flow are deliberate.
First, the escalation from the cheap text layer to the expensive vision call is page-local and demand-driven: the renderer is imported lazily and invoked only for pages below the 50-character threshold, so a born-digital document with a clean text layer never loads the rasterizer at all, and the number of paid \gls{OCR} calls stays proportional to the count of genuinely scanned pages rather than to document length.
Second, the document-level guard distinguishes a partially failed document from an empty one for a concrete reason: ingested documents are deduplicated by content hash, so silently recording a zero-text document as ingested would cause its hash to be remembered and the document to be skipped forever, permanently hiding the failure.
Raising instead leaves the document outside the corpus, where a later run --- after the loop is diagnosed or the source is replaced --- can retry it.

\textbf{Result.}
The pipeline ingests both structured and unstructured official sources end to end.
The canonical store holds 136 admission records and 699 cutoff records for HUST and 20 admission records and 16 cutoff records for VNU-UET, and the knowledge corpus holds 93 documents and 692 chunks drawn from HUST, NEU, VNU-UET, and national \gls{MOET} regulations, as reported in Section~\ref{section:4.3}.
The degenerate-output safeguard is what makes the scanned subset of that corpus ingestible: without it, a single repetition-loop page would either bloat the store with megabytes of junk or abort the document; with it, the bad page is isolated and the rest of the document is preserved.

\section{Unified observability over advisory runs}
\label{section:5.4}

\textbf{Problem.}
An advisory run is opaque from the outside: one consultation fans out into six pipeline stages and several model calls, some of which retry or fall back, and when a run is slow, expensive, or wrong there is no single place to see why.
An earlier iteration of the system addressed part of this by writing per-stage events to its own database table and rendering them in an in-app panel (Section~\ref{section:3.6}), but that view showed stage timings only --- not the prompt, the raw response, the token usage, or the per-attempt retry and fallback behavior of the underlying model calls, which is exactly the information needed to diagnose a degraded or costly run.
Maintaining a bespoke trace store and viewer also carried its own code and test burden for a capability that purpose-built tools already provide.

\textbf{Solution.}
The bespoke trace store and its in-app panel were retired in favor of a single observability sink, Langfuse, integrated through a dedicated \texttt{observability} package.
A run opens one root span keyed by a trace identifier derived deterministically from the run's \texttt{trace\_run\_id}, so the external trace and the database run cross-reference by the same integer, and the span's session identifier is set to the chat session token so every run of one conversation groups together.
The same \texttt{traced} decorator that wraps each advisory node (Section~\ref{section:4.1}) now opens a child span per pipeline stage, so the fixed graph of Section~\ref{section:3.3} yields a trace tree of identical shape on every run.
Crucially, each model call is recorded as a generation observation carrying the model name, the raw system and user prompts, the raw output, the token usage, the latency, and the attempt number, the \texttt{used\_fallback} flag, and the \texttt{failure\_type} of Section~\ref{section:5.2} --- and because each retry and fallback is a separate generation, the resilience behavior of the gateway becomes directly visible.
Capturing token usage required a small change to the inference path: the Gemini provider, which previously discarded everything but the response text, now reads the response's usage metadata into an optional \texttt{usage} field on the inference result, from which the generation's token counts are populated.
The integration preserves the graceful-degradation discipline of the rest of the system: the client is disabled by default and built lazily, a missing client makes every helper a no-op, and any backend error is caught and logged rather than allowed to break a run, so observability can never become a new failure mode.
Nesting is automatic rather than threaded through function arguments: because a run executes synchronously on one worker thread, the root span, the stage spans, and the generations share that thread's tracing context and nest correctly without passing a trace identifier between layers.

\textbf{Result.}
Every advisory run is now a single queryable trace --- a tree of six stage spans with the model calls recorded beneath the stages that make them --- that exposes the prompts, outputs, token usage, latency, and retry or fallback path the earlier in-app panel could not.
Consolidating on one external sink removed the parallel trace store and viewer while adding the per-call and cost visibility that was previously absent, and self-hosting keeps the captured prompts and student messages on the same machine as the rest of the system, consistent with the anonymity posture of NFR-03.

\section{Declarative orchestration of the conversation and knowledge flows}
\label{section:5.5}

\textbf{Problem.}
While the advisory pipeline was a declarative graph from the start (Section~\ref{section:3.3}), the surrounding orchestration was not.
A conversation turn was an imperative chain --- extract profile, run deterministic guards for reset, continuation, and correction, classify intent, then branch into one of several handlers --- whose control flow lived implicitly in nested conditionals rather than in an inspectable structure.
The knowledge question-answering logic had a second problem: the same embed--retrieve--augment--gate--generate sequence was reached from three different call sites (the inline turn, the batch fan-out, and the hybrid comparison), inviting divergence and giving those paths no uniform place to be traced.

\textbf{Solution.}
The conversation turn and the knowledge flow were expressed as LangGraph graphs, deliberately without adding any run-time model autonomy, so the determinism argued for in Section~\ref{section:3.3} is preserved.
The turn graph makes the guards conditional-edge nodes and the intent router a routing node that directs each message to a handler node --- enqueue an advisory run, answer a knowledge question, run a hybrid comparison, hold a conversational reply, ask for clarification, or decline an out-of-scope request --- so the routes the system distinguishes are now visible as graph edges rather than buried in branches.
The knowledge flow is defined once as a reusable subgraph (embed, retrieve school-scoped chunks, augment with national chunks, gate on confidence, generate) hidden behind the knowledge service's unchanged \texttt{answer} method, so all three callers share a single definition and the confidence gate of Algorithm~\ref{alg:retrieval} cannot drift between them.
The advisory graph itself is unchanged and still runs on the background worker; the turn graph only enqueues advisory runs, keeping the durable-queue design of Section~\ref{section:4.2} intact.
Because every node --- in the turn graph, the knowledge subgraph, and the advisory graph --- is wrapped by the same tracing decorator, the unified observability of Section~\ref{section:5.4} extends across all three flows uniformly.
The refactoring was guarded by characterization tests that pin the turn outcome for every route before the structure changed, so the behavior is preserved rather than merely re-expressed.

\textbf{Result.}
The system's control flow is now declarative end to end: the per-turn routing, the knowledge subgraph, and the advisory pipeline are all graphs over typed state, and the knowledge subgraph is defined once and reused by the inline, fan-out, and hybrid paths.
Uniform node wrapping means the observability of Section~\ref{section:5.4} now covers the synchronous turn and knowledge paths that the run-only tracer previously missed, and the full test suite of Section~\ref{section:4.4} passing after the change is the evidence that the externally observable behavior is unchanged.

\section{Version-gated semantic answer cache}
\label{section:5.6}

\textbf{Problem.}
Factual questions cluster heavily: many students ask the same few things (``how much is tuition?'', ``what majors does this school offer?''), and answering each one re-embeds the question, runs a vector search, and calls the model to generate a grounded answer.
Repeating that work for a question already answered spends inference quota and adds latency for no new information.
A naive cache is unsafe here, though, because the knowledge corpus is re-ingested during the admission season (NFR-02), so a cached answer can outlive the document it was drawn from and become wrong.

\textbf{Solution.}
The knowledge service is fronted by a semantic cache (migration \texttt{019}, Section~\ref{section:4.2}) that reuses a past answer when a new question is close enough, while gating every reuse on freshness.
On a miss, the answer is stored together with the question's 768-dimensional embedding, its confidence, and a stamp recording the version of its data scope at the time of writing; the store is indexed for vector search with the same \gls{HNSW} machinery as the main corpus (Section~\ref{section:3.2}).
On a new question, the cache embeds it, finds the nearest stored entry within the same school-and-topic scope, and accepts it only when the similarity clears a threshold \textit{and} the entry's stamped scope version still equals the current one; otherwise it is treated as a miss and the question flows through to the subgraph of Section~\ref{section:5.5}.
Freshness is enforced by bumping a scope's version on every ingest, which invalidates exactly the cached answers whose data may have changed, so the cache trades staleness for speed only while the underlying documents are unchanged.
Like every other external-dependency feature, the cache is disabled by default and is a drop-in seam: when it is off the service behaves exactly as before, and the result carries a flag indicating whether it came from the cache.

\textbf{Result.}
When enabled, a repeated factual question is answered from the cache without an embedding-conditioned model call, and a re-ingest of the relevant scope correctly forces the next identical question to be recomputed, so the speed-up never comes at the cost of serving an answer the corpus no longer supports.
The hit/miss path, the similarity gate, and stale-invalidation-on-ingest are covered by integration tests in the suite of Section~\ref{section:4.4}; a measured hit rate is left to a production deployment, since it depends on a live question stream the development corpus does not contain.

This chapter examined the system's contributions in depth.
Three address gaps that existing admission tools leave open: conflict-aware consolidation that preserves and discloses contradictory source data, a resilient inference gateway that distinguishes hard from soft model failures and keeps the assistant available through both, and an end-to-end ingestion pipeline that handles heterogeneous and scanned official sources with an explicit safeguard against model \gls{OCR} failure.
Three concern the engineering of the application itself: unified observability that makes every advisory run a single queryable trace, the declarative orchestration of the conversation and knowledge flows as reusable graphs, and a version-gated semantic cache that reuses past answers without serving stale ones.
Together the first three address the gaps --- unreconciled data, unreliable model dependence, and inaccessible source formats --- that Chapter~\ref{chapter:Related_works} identified, while the latter three make the system observable, maintainable, and economical to operate.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:conclusion} concludes the thesis and sets out the remaining work.
